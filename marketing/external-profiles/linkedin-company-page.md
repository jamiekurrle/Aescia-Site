## About (1,200–1,500 chars)

> Aescia builds a continuous-care platform for the weeks between a high-acuity discharge and the next clinical appointment.
>
> Aescia for Hospitals is an investigational software-as-a-medical-device for structured post-discharge monitoring, intended to be classified Class IIa under TGA Rule 3.4. A regulatory submission has not yet been lodged. The product is in clinical evaluation through SAFE-Discharge, a 550-patient prospective single-centre trial at the Royal Prince Alfred Hospital cardiothoracic surgical unit in Sydney (ACTRN12625001425482), under the principal investigator Dr Kei Woldendorp of The Baird Institute.
>
> Aescia for Clinics is a workflow and patient-preparation platform for specialty clinics. It is not a medical device. It delivers clinician-authored prep pathways, multichannel reminders, and structured exports back to the chart, so that high-volume endoscopy, infusion, and procedural lists run with less phone work and fewer cancelled appointments. Pricing is negotiated per clinic; design partners start free until the platform proves itself.
>
> One pathway engine underneath both products. Five step types — Collect, Follow, Remind, Educate, Export. Designed to add a signal layer for the team, not a new portal for the patient.
>
> Aescia is incorporated as Aescia Pty Ltd (AU) and 9550-0708 Québec inc (CA), with operations in Sydney and Montréal. The company is a District 3 portfolio company at Concordia University and is enrolled in the Medical Technology Association of Australia's MedTech Compass programme.

### Post 3 — The post-discharge problem (Monitor angle)

> The first thirty days after a high-acuity surgical discharge is the part of the patient's pathway that is least observed.
>
> Inside the hospital, the patient is watched continuously. After discharge, watchfulness reverts to phone calls, paper instructions, and whichever follow-up appointment the system can fit them into. The peer-reviewed literature on this gap is consistent across two decades: a meaningful fraction of 30-day readmissions are avoidable, and structured contact within that window is one of the few interventions that helps.
>
> Aescia for Hospitals is built to make that interval observable. Daily check-ins, clinician-authored rule sets, and prioritisation that produces one ranked list for the unit — not a new inbox for the nurse. The product is investigational and in clinical evaluation through the SAFE-Discharge trial at Royal Prince Alfred Hospital, with cardiothoracic surgery as the first indication. The secondary endpoint is alert-burden: fewer than one nurse-actionable flag per patient per week over the 30-day window.
>
> A regulatory submission has not yet been lodged. The product is not approved, listed, cleared, or certified in any jurisdiction. We will publish what the trial shows, whatever it shows.

### Post 4 — The specialty-clinic workflow problem (Clinic angle)

> A specialty endoscopy list rarely fails because of the procedure. It fails earlier — somewhere between the booking call, the prep instruction sheet, the patient's diabetes regimen, and the GLP-1 they didn't mention.
>
> Aescia for Clinics replaces the parts of that pathway that currently live on phone trees and printed sheets. Clinician-authored prep, branched for diabetes, anticoagulation, and GLP-1 peri-procedural overlay. Multichannel reminders that respect the way the patient actually checks their messages. Structured export back to the chart, so the next clinician does not have to ask the same five questions twice.
>
> Aescia for Clinics is a workflow tool. It is not a medical device, it does not propose clinical decisions, and it does not diagnose. It delivers clinician-authored content and routes exceptions to named staff. Pricing is negotiated per clinic; design partners start free until the platform proves itself. The economic case is subtractive: less reception phone work, fewer repeat visits driven by inadequate prep, fewer recall patients lost.
>
> The MVP is feature-complete. Production deployment follows once the database and EMR integration targets land for the first paying clinic.

### Post 5 — Founder voice

> I trained as a critical-care physician across Montréal and Sydney, and the thing I kept noticing was always the same.
>
> Patients I admitted at three in the morning were watched continuously, second-by-second, for as long as they were on the unit. The instant they discharged, that watchfulness reverted to phone calls and paper instructions and whichever clinic could fit them in three weeks later. The ones who came back tended to come back inside the first month, and the warning signs had usually shown up days before.
>
> Aescia is what happens when you take that observation and refuse to ship a generic patient app over the top of it. We work in two products because the discharge problem and the specialty-clinic-prep problem both live in the same gap, but they have different regulatory shapes — one is a software-as-a-medical-device, the other is a workflow tool that is explicitly not a medical device. We built one engine and applied it twice.
>
> We are early. The trial is running, the first paying clinic is shipping, and we have a small operating team across Montréal and Sydney. More to come. — James Kurrle, founder, Aescia.
